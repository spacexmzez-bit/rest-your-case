Start here

The basic ideas behind every defense, explained without legal training.

Burden of proof & reasonable doubt

The prosecution must prove every required part of the charge beyond a reasonable doubt. The defense does not have to prove innocence. A reasonable doubt comes from a real weakness in the evidence, rather than an imaginary possibility.

How to use itIdentify the exact part of the charge the evidence fails to prove. Connect your argument to testimony or records.

Example in practice

A blurry video shows someone in a common jacket. Explain why it does not reliably identify your client.

Elements & mental state (mens rea)

Elements are the separate facts needed to prove a crime. Mental state means what the person intended, knew, or consciously risked. Causing harm alone may not prove the charged mental state.

How to use itMake a checklist of the required conduct, mental state, and result. Test each against the evidence.

Example in practice

An incorrect invoice may be a mistake. Ask what proves it was a deliberate attempt to cheat someone.

Self-defense & who attacked first

A person may be legally justified in using force against an imminent unlawful threat. Necessity, proportionality, and rules about retreat matter. Deadly force has stricter limits. The case's law controls.

How to use itReconstruct who threatened whom, what danger existed, and whether the response was necessary.

Example in practice

Video shows an attacker raising a knife before your client reacts. Use that sequence to support a defensive response.

Excluding illegally obtained evidence

A motion to suppress asks the judge to keep evidence out because it was obtained unlawfully. Related evidence may also be affected. Exceptions can apply, so an unlawful search does not automatically erase the entire case.

How to use itIdentify your client's protected interest, the police action, and the evidence connected to it.

Example in practice

An officer searches a private bag without consent or another lawful basis. Ask to suppress what that search uncovered.

Discovery, Brady & Giglio disclosure

Discovery is the exchange of case information. Brady concerns material evidence favorable to the defense; Giglio includes information that undermines a prosecution witness, such as promised benefits. A remedy depends on timing and harm.

How to use itRequest the missing material, explain how it affects the defense, and seek an appropriate remedy such as more preparation time. A mistrial is not automatic.

Example in practice

An informant was promised a reduced sentence. Request the deal and use it to question the informant's motive.

Foundation, authentication & admission

Foundation explains why evidence may be used. Authentication shows that an item is what its presenter claims. Admission is the judge's decision to allow it into evidence; marking an exhibit only labels it.

How to use itIn RUC, mark the item, establish its origin and reliability through a witness or records, then ask the judge to admit it.

Example in practice

Have the camera owner identify the recording and explain how it was saved before asking to admit the video.

Impeachment: testing credibility

Impeachment means showing a reason to doubt a witness. It can involve a changed story, a motive to lie, or poor observation. Discrediting one witness does not automatically disprove every fact.

How to use itUse a specific statement or record and let the witness respond. Separate what they observed from what they inferred.

Example in practice

The witness now says the car was red; their earlier statement says they could not see its color. Put the two accounts side by side.

Objections, motions & rulings

An objection challenges a question or evidence when it is offered. A motion asks the judge for a decision, such as excluding evidence. Sustained means the judge agrees with an objection; overruled means the judge rejects it.

How to use itState the legal ground briefly. If an improper answer is already given, ask to strike it and request an instruction when useful.

Example in practice

Say: “Objection, hearsay.” If the witness has already repeated the rumor, ask the judge to strike that answer.

Courtroom objections

Recognize the problem, state the ground, and ask for a focused ruling.

FRE 801–802

Hearsay

Hearsay is an out-of-court statement offered to prove what it says is true. It can be spoken or written. Some statements are excluded from this definition, and others qualify for exceptions.

How to use itAsk what the statement is being used to prove and whether an exclusion or exception applies.

Example in practice

A witness says, “My neighbor told me Alex stole the car.” If offered to prove Alex stole it, object to hearsay.

FRE 803(1)–(2); 801(d)(2)

Common hearsay exceptions & opposing-party statements

A description made while observing an event or immediately after it may be a present sense impression. A statement made under the stress of a startling event may be an excited utterance. An opposing party's own statement offered against them is treated as non-hearsay, not an exception.

How to use itCheck timing, personal observation, continuing stress, and who is offering whose statement. A label alone is not enough.

Example in practice

A calm retelling the next day is not automatically an excited utterance. Ask when it was said and whether the speaker was still under the event's stress.

FRE 611(c)

Leading questions on direct examination

A leading question suggests its own answer. They are ordinarily restricted when a lawyer questions their own friendly witness, but are ordinarily allowed on cross-examination and in some other circumstances.

How to use itObject to an improper suggested answer on direct. Avoid treating all leading questions as misconduct.

Example in practice

The prosecutor asks their friendly witness, “You saw Alex holding the knife, correct?” Ask for an open question such as “What did you see?”

FRE 602

Personal knowledge & speculation

A fact witness generally must have personally perceived what they describe. Guessing another person's thoughts or filling gaps with assumptions is not the same as observing facts.

How to use itAsk how the witness knows. Object when there is no basis in their own perception.

Example in practice

A neighbor claims Alex intended to kill, but only saw Alex walk past. Ask what they actually saw or heard.

FRE 404(b); 403

Prior bad acts & improper character evidence

A past bad act generally cannot be used simply to say the defendant is a bad person who probably did this too. Other uses, such as motive, identity, or intent, may be allowed when their requirements are met.

How to use itChallenge the actual purpose, relevance, notice where required, and unfair prejudice. Do not claim all prior-act evidence is forbidden.

Example in practice

The prosecutor says an old unrelated arrest proves your client is a thief. Object to using past conduct to prove present guilt.

FRE 403

Unfair prejudice

Useful evidence is often damaging. Unfair prejudice means a risk of an improper emotional or other unfair decision. Exclusion may be warranted when that risk substantially outweighs the evidence's usefulness.

How to use itExplain the unfair risk and why a less inflammatory version would prove the same point.

Example in practice

Several graphic photographs add little beyond one clear image. Ask the judge to limit needless repetition.

FRE 611(a)

Assumes facts not in evidence

A loaded question treats a disputed fact as already established. The judge controls questioning so a witness is not forced to accept an unsupported premise.

How to use itName the assumption and request a question without it.

Example in practice

“Where did you hide the stolen money?” assumes the witness stole money. Ask the prosecutor to establish that premise first.

FRE 611(a)

Compound questions

One question asks several things at once. A yes or no answer may be unclear because the witness agrees with one part but disputes another.

How to use itAsk for one question at a time.

Example in practice

“Did you take the bag and erase the camera recording?” combines two disputed acts. Ask that they be separated.

FRE 611(a)

Argumentative or repetitive questioning

Questions should obtain useful testimony. Arguing with a witness, harassing them, or repeating a fully answered question may call for the judge to control the examination.

How to use itIdentify the argument or repetition and ask the judge to require a factual question.

Example in practice

After an answer, the lawyer says, “So you expect this jury to believe that ridiculous story?” Object to argumentative questioning.

FRE 701–702

Ordinary witnesses giving expert conclusions

An ordinary witness can give some opinions based on their own perceptions, such as apparent speed. Specialized scientific conclusions need a proper expert basis; a job title alone does not supply it.

How to use itSeparate observations from conclusions. Ask about qualifications and the method used.

Example in practice

A neighbor calls a wound “proof of deliberate execution.” Ask for the observed facts and object to unsupported medical conclusions.

FRE 702

Expert reliability & a Daubert challenge

Expert opinions need useful specialized knowledge, sufficient information, and reliably applied methods. A Daubert challenge asks the judge to examine that reliability. Disagreement with a result alone does not prove unreliability.

How to use itExamine qualifications, validation, error rates, controls, calibration, and application to this case.

Example in practice

The analyst used an unvalidated method without a control sample. Request a reliability hearing before the opinion reaches the jury.

Sixth Amendment; Crawford

Confronting an absent accuser

In criminal cases, testimonial statements offered for their truth usually cannot replace a witness whom the defense has no opportunity to question. Generally, an unavailable witness also requires a prior opportunity for cross-examination; exceptions exist.

How to use itDistinguish a hearsay objection from the constitutional right to question an accuser.

Example in practice

A detective repeats an absent informant's formal accusation to establish guilt. Raise hearsay and the lack of an opportunity to question the informant.

FRE 401–402

Relevance

Evidence is relevant if it makes a fact that matters in the case more or less likely. Interesting details that do not help decide a disputed issue can distract the jury.

How to use itExplain the connection between the evidence and a required element or credibility issue.

Example in practice

A witness is asked about an unrelated hobby. Ask how that helps establish any fact in dispute.

Charges & what must be proved

Every charge in the case data, grouped by case type. These are RUC summaries; exact legal elements depend on the governing law.

Violent Crimes

First-Degree Murder

An unlawful intentional killing carried out with advance reflection or planning.

What the charge requires in RUC

  • Intent to kill
  • Advance reflection or planning
  • A killing without lawful justification

How to use itTest the timeline for actual planning and investigate identity and self-defense.

Example in practice

Messages show a meeting was arranged, but no plan to kill. Explain why arranging the meeting alone does not prove premeditation.

Violent Crimes

Second-Degree Murder

An unlawful killing with the required malice but without first-degree premeditation. RUC includes intentional killings and extreme disregard for life.

What the charge requires in RUC

  • A killing linked to the defendant
  • Intentional lethal harm or extreme disregard for life
  • No lawful justification

How to use itDistinguish deliberate harm from accident, legally sufficient mitigation, or justified force.

Example in practice

The prosecution alleges a planned killing but has no planning evidence. Examine whether the remaining evidence proves the mental state for any lesser charge.

Violent Crimes

Aggravated Assault with a Deadly Weapon

In RUC's version, using a dangerous weapon to make someone reasonably fear immediate serious injury.

What the charge requires in RUC

  • An act causing reasonable fear of immediate serious injury
  • Use or display of a dangerous weapon
  • The required intent

How to use itCheck the threat, distance, ability to act, and any justification.

Example in practice

A witness saw a tool in your client's hand but heard no threat. Ask what created a reasonable fear of immediate injury.

Violent Crimes

Armed Robbery

Taking property from someone or their immediate presence through force or threats while armed.

What the charge requires in RUC

  • Taking someone else's property
  • Force or intimidation
  • A dangerous weapon
  • Intent to permanently deprive the owner

How to use itTest identity, whether a taking occurred, and whether force accompanied it.

Example in practice

A disputed debt does not automatically justify taking a wallet. Focus on evidence of ownership, intent, and the alleged threat.

Violent Crimes

Kidnapping / False Imprisonment

Unlawfully moving or confining someone against their will. Movement and confinement requirements differ between offenses and jurisdictions.

What the charge requires in RUC

  • Restraint or qualifying movement
  • Lack of consent
  • Force, fear, or deception
  • Knowing restraint

How to use itExamine exits, duration, threats, and the person's ability to leave.

Example in practice

The door was open and the person freely left. Use that evidence to test whether they were actually confined.

Violent Crimes

Aggravated Battery Causing Great Bodily Harm

Unlawful physical contact causing serious injury. RUC focuses on intentional severe harm.

What the charge requires in RUC

  • Unlawful physical contact
  • Serious or lasting injury
  • The required intent and a link to that injury

How to use itCompare medical records with the alleged mechanism of injury and examine justified force.

Example in practice

Hospital notes describe a minor bruise. Ask what evidence supports the allegation of severe injury.

Violent Crimes

Attempted Murder

Acting with an intent to kill and taking a substantial or direct step beyond preparation toward that killing.

What the charge requires in RUC

  • Specific intent to kill
  • An act beyond mere preparation
  • An attempted unlawful killing

How to use itSeparate an intent to scare or injure from an intent to kill.

Example in practice

A threat is disturbing, but the prosecution must also identify the required act toward carrying out a killing.

Financial Fraud & White-Collar Crimes

Wire Fraud (18 U.S.C. § 1343)

Deliberately using an interstate electronic communication to help a scheme to obtain money or property through significant deception.

What the charge requires in RUC

  • A fraudulent scheme for money or property
  • Intent to defraud
  • A meaningful deception
  • An interstate wire communication advancing the scheme

How to use itLook for evidence of deliberate deception, rather than assuming a failed business or accounting error proves fraud.

Example in practice

Emails show the seller corrected a mistaken invoice before payment. Use them to test the claimed dishonest intent.

Financial Fraud & White-Collar Crimes

Securities Fraud / Insider Trading

Deception connected to securities, or trading on important secret information in breach of a relevant duty. RUC combines several legal theories.

What the charge requires in RUC

  • Important deception or nonpublic information
  • The required dishonest mental state
  • A securities transaction connection
  • A relevant duty for the insider-trading theory

How to use itIdentify the specific theory and whether the information was important, nonpublic, and used in breach of a duty.

Example in practice

The supposedly secret earnings figure was published before the trade. Compare publication and trading times.

Financial Fraud & White-Collar Crimes

Embezzlement & Corporate Misappropriation

Dishonestly using property for an unauthorized purpose after being trusted with it.

What the charge requires in RUC

  • Property entrusted to the defendant
  • Unauthorized dishonest conversion or transfer
  • Required intent

How to use itTrace authorization, account access, and the final destination of the funds.

Example in practice

The transfer went to an approved supplier account. Request the approval record before assuming personal misuse.

Cybercrime & Digital Intrusion

Unauthorized Computer Access (CFAA - 18 U.S.C. § 1030)

Intentionally entering a protected computer or accessing information beyond permitted access under the relevant CFAA provision. Misusing information one may access is not automatically the same as unauthorized access.

What the charge requires in RUC

  • A protected computer
  • The required unauthorized access
  • Required knowledge or intent
  • The information or other result required by the charged provision

How to use itIdentify the exact access boundary and link the activity to a person. Shared credentials or an IP address alone may be inconclusive.

Example in practice

Logs show a shared administrator account. Ask who could use it and what independently ties this session to your client.

Cybercrime & Digital Intrusion

Digital Extortion & Ransomware Deployment

Using threats to lock, damage, or expose data to obtain money or another thing of value.

What the charge requires in RUC

  • A relevant threat or malicious locking activity
  • A demand for value
  • Intent to extort
  • A reliable connection to the defendant

How to use itSeparate the attacker, the affected account, and the person receiving payment.

Example in practice

A ransom note bears your client's username. Investigate whether that account was compromised before treating the label as attribution.

Cybercrime & Digital Intrusion

Aggravated Identity Theft (18 U.S.C. § 1028A)

Knowingly using, possessing, or transferring another real person's identifying information without lawful authority during and in relation to a qualifying listed felony.

What the charge requires in RUC

  • Another actual person's identifying information
  • Knowledge it belongs to another person
  • Lack of lawful authority
  • A qualifying underlying felony and the required connection

How to use itCheck whose identity was used, what the defendant knew, and whether the identity use meets the charged felony connection.

Example in practice

A synthetic test identity is alleged to be a real person's identity. Request evidence identifying that person and showing the defendant knew.

Cybercrime & Digital Intrusion

Trade Secret Theft & Corporate Espionage

Knowingly taking or copying a protected business secret without permission, with the economic intent and other requirements of the charged offense.

What the charge requires in RUC

  • Information that qualifies as a trade secret
  • Unauthorized taking or copying
  • Required economic purpose and knowledge
  • A connection to the accused

How to use itCheck secrecy measures, public availability, permission, and the purpose of any copying.

Example in practice

The copied document was already on the company's public website. Ask what protected secret was actually taken.

Substance Use, Trafficking & Controlled Substances

Possession with Intent to Distribute (PWID)

Knowingly possessing a controlled drug with the purpose of distributing it. Possession and intent to distribute are separate issues.

What the charge requires in RUC

  • A controlled substance
  • Knowing possession or control
  • Intent to distribute

How to use itTest control of the drugs and the evidence of distribution. Quantity and packaging are clues, rather than automatic answers.

Example in practice

Drugs were in a shared car with no sales messages. Ask what proves your client's knowledge, control, and distribution intent.

Substance Use, Trafficking & Controlled Substances

Conspiracy to Traffic Narcotics (21 U.S.C. § 846)

Knowingly joining an agreement to commit a federal drug offense. Mere association is insufficient. Section 846 does not require proof of a separate overt act.

What the charge requires in RUC

  • An agreement to commit the relevant drug offense
  • Knowledge of the unlawful objective
  • Voluntary participation

How to use itDistinguish knowing membership from friendship, presence, or an innocent delivery. Interstate movement is not a universal requirement.

Example in practice

Your client drove a friend without knowing the sealed parcel's contents. Ask what proves they knowingly joined the drug agreement.

Substance Use, Trafficking & Controlled Substances

Operating a Drug-Involved Premises

Knowingly maintaining or making a place available for prohibited drug activities under the charged provision.

What the charge requires in RUC

  • The relevant management, maintenance, or control of a place
  • Required knowledge
  • The prohibited drug purpose or use

How to use itLook at knowledge, actual authority, and the use of the property; ownership alone does not answer every question.

Example in practice

The landlord learned of the activity only after police arrived. Examine notices and messages for prior knowledge.

Substance Use, Trafficking & Controlled Substances

Illicit Pharmaceutical & Precursor Diversion

Knowingly diverting controlled medicines or regulated production chemicals from lawful channels into unlawful use or supply.

What the charge requires in RUC

  • Regulated substances
  • An unlawful obtaining, diversion, or distribution
  • Required knowledge or fraud
  • A link to the defendant

How to use itAudit prescriptions, authorization, stock records, and who could alter the inventory.

Example in practice

Missing stock is attributed to a pharmacist, but several people share the login. Compare access logs and physical handling records.

Substance Use, Trafficking & Controlled Substances

Simple Felony Possession

Knowing possession of a controlled drug without lawful authorization, charged as a felony where the applicable law supports it. Classification does not always depend only on weight.

What the charge requires in RUC

  • A controlled substance
  • Knowing physical possession or control
  • No lawful authorization
  • The facts required for the charged felony classification

How to use itCheck laboratory identification, knowledge, control, lawful prescriptions, and the actual classification rule.

Example in practice

A bag was under another passenger's seat. Ask what proves your client's knowledge and ability to control it.

Overcharge Scenarios & Manslaughter

Voluntary Manslaughter (Heat of Passion / Imperfect Self-Defense)

An intentional unlawful killing that may receive a lesser classification because of legally adequate sudden provocation or a recognized imperfect self-defense doctrine.

What the charge requires in RUC

  • An intentional killing
  • Qualifying provocation without sufficient cooling time or recognized imperfect self-defense
  • A causal connection between the mitigating circumstances and the killing

How to use itInvestigate the immediate sequence and governing law. Anger or an unreasonable fear does not automatically qualify.

Example in practice

Video shows an immediate response to a serious confrontation. Use the timing to investigate whether legally sufficient mitigation applies.

Overcharge Scenarios & Manslaughter

Involuntary Manslaughter / Gross Criminal Negligence

An unintended death caused by the level of recklessness or serious negligence required by the applicable law.

What the charge requires in RUC

  • A death caused by the defendant's conduct or legally required omission
  • Required recklessness or gross negligence
  • No intent to kill for this theory

How to use itSeparate a tragic accident from a grossly dangerous departure from reasonable care.

Example in practice

A machine failed despite documented maintenance. Examine whether the defendant ignored a known serious danger.

Overcharge Scenarios & Manslaughter

Vehicular Manslaughter with Gross Negligence

Causing death through driving that meets the required serious negligence, recklessness, or impairment standard.

What the charge requires in RUC

  • Operation of a vehicle
  • The charged dangerous or impaired conduct
  • A causal link to the death
  • Required mental state

How to use itUse collision reconstruction, mechanical records, and toxicology to test both fault and causation.

Example in practice

A documented brake defect may explain the collision. Compare it with the allegation that reckless driving caused the death.

Overcharge Scenarios & Manslaughter

Prosecutorial Overcharge: Felony Murder Elevation

Some laws allow a murder charge for a death connected to a qualifying felony, even without a separate intent to kill. Liability limits for accomplices vary. Overcharge describes a disputed charging theory, rather than a separate crime.

What the charge requires in RUC

  • A qualifying underlying felony
  • A death with the required connection to it
  • The defendant's legally sufficient participation
  • Any additional limits imposed by the applicable law

How to use itTest the qualifying felony, connection to the death, and rules for this defendant's role. Lack of an intent to kill alone may not defeat felony murder.

Example in practice

A getaway driver had no knowledge of lethal conduct. Examine whether the governing law requires additional participation or mental-state proof.

Overcharge Scenarios & Manslaughter

Reckless Endangerment Resulting in Death

Consciously disregarding a substantial unjustified danger that leads to death, under the offense used in the case.

What the charge requires in RUC

  • A substantial unjustified risk
  • Required awareness and disregard of that risk
  • A death caused by the conduct

How to use itShow what danger was known, what precautions were taken, and what actually caused the death.

Example in practice

A supervisor received no warning of a hidden structural fault. Test the allegation that they consciously ignored a known danger.

Police actions & evidence challenges

All eight legal flaws in the case data, translated into practical defense questions.

4th Amendment / Reasonable Suspicion

Anonymous tips & the basis for a stop

An anonymous accusation needs enough reliability and supporting detail to justify the police action taken. A stop and a search have different legal requirements.

How to use itAsk what police verified before acting, and whether it showed suspected wrongdoing rather than just a person's appearance.

Example in practice

A caller names a jacket color but gives no reliable account of a crime. Compare the tip with what officers actually observed.

Due Process / Lineup Bias

Suggestive identification

Police identification procedures can steer a witness toward a suspect. Constitutional exclusion depends on the procedure and reliability; an imperfect identification is not automatically excluded.

How to use itExamine instructions, alternatives, lighting, original descriptions, and police influence.

Example in practice

Police present your client alone and say, “We caught the robber.” Ask how that suggestion affected the identification.

4th Amendment / Voluntariness

Disputed consent to search

Consent must be voluntary, come from someone with appropriate authority, and stay within its permitted scope. Pressure or a limited permission may matter.

How to use itCheck recordings for the exact words, circumstances, authority, and any withdrawal of consent.

Example in practice

Your client allowed an officer to look in the trunk, but refused a search of a locked case. Compare the search with that limit.

4th Amendment / Particularity Clause

Search beyond a warrant

A warrant limits the place to search and items to seek. The size of the item sought can limit which containers may be searched. Exceptions may permit some other actions.

How to use itRead the warrant and compare each search step with its scope and any claimed exception.

Example in practice

A warrant for a stolen television is used to open a tiny pill bottle. Ask how the listed object could fit inside.

FRE 901 / Evidence Integrity

Chain of custody & authenticity

Custody records track an item's handling. A gap may raise doubts about identity, alteration, or contamination. Not every gap prevents admission; some affect how much the jury should trust the item.

How to use itFind the missing transfer and explain a concrete risk. Distinguish authenticity from constitutional suppression.

Example in practice

A sample changes labels between collection and testing. Ask who relabeled it and how the lab knows it tested the original sample.

5th Amendment / Miranda Violation

Questioning after rights are invoked

Miranda applies to custodial interrogation. An unambiguous request for counsel generally requires questioning to stop under the applicable rules. Invoking silence has related but distinct rules; admissibility and exceptions need analysis.

How to use itReconstruct custody, warnings, the exact invocation, and who restarted the conversation.

Example in practice

A recording captures “I want a lawyer,” followed by continued interrogation. Seek to exclude the resulting statement on the appropriate ground.

Due Process / Brady Violation

Hidden witness agreements

Promises of leniency or other benefits can help the defense test a witness's credibility. Material favorable information cannot be concealed simply because it is embarrassing to the prosecution.

How to use itRequest the agreement and related promises, then identify how they affect the witness's motive and your preparation.

Example in practice

A witness denies receiving anything, but a letter promises a reduced charge. Ask for disclosure and confront the contradiction.

FRE 702 / Daubert Challenge

Unreliable scientific methods

A scientific-sounding result still needs a reliable method applied correctly to sufficient information.

How to use itRequest validation studies, raw results, controls, and calibration records. Ask for a Rule 702 reliability ruling when supported.

Example in practice

A lab reports a match using a method it never validated. Ask the judge to assess whether that opinion has a reliable basis.

Witness credibility

All witness frictions and client complications: evaluate facts without turning suspicion into proof.

Poor observation conditions

Distance, darkness, noise, intoxication, stress, or impaired senses can limit what a witness accurately perceived.

How to use itAsk about specific conditions and compare them with the confidence of the identification.

Example in practice

A witness watched from across a dark road without glasses. Establish distance and lighting before challenging certainty.

Grudges & prior disputes

A personal feud may give a witness a reason to exaggerate or blame someone. Bias does not itself prove the witness is lying.

How to use itEstablish the relationship using specific events or records, then compare the account with independent evidence.

Example in practice

Messages show a recent threat to “get even.” Ask about the dispute and check whether other evidence supports the accusation.

Financial self-interest

A witness may protect income, avoid repayment, or shift financial responsibility by blaming the accused.

How to use itFind the benefit or loss at stake and connect it to the testimony.

Example in practice

A manager could owe repayment if their own error caused the loss. Examine whether blaming your client avoids that responsibility.

Changed stories

A prior inconsistent statement may weaken reliability. Differences may also have innocent explanations.

How to use itIdentify the exact earlier statement, circumstances, and contradiction; give the witness a chance to explain.

Example in practice

The first interview says “I heard a bang”; trial testimony says “I saw the shot.” Ask what explains the added observation.

Cooperating co-defendants & plea deals

A co-defendant may gain a lighter sentence or other benefit by helping prosecutors. The deal is relevant to credibility, not automatic proof of fabrication.

How to use itAsk what was promised, what remains at risk, and whether independent evidence supports the account.

Example in practice

A witness hopes testimony will reduce a ten-year sentence. Establish that incentive before discussing their accusation.

Career or professional pressure

A person facing discipline, licensing trouble, or blame at work may have a reason to protect their reputation.

How to use itLook for documented pressure and compare the testimony with contemporaneous records.

Example in practice

An analyst could be disciplined for mishandling a sample. Use the handling log to test their denial of an error.

Flight for an unrelated warrant

Running may reflect fear of an unrelated arrest rather than guilt of the charged crime. Flight is a fact to interpret in context.

How to use itInvestigate the alternative reason with records, while checking what your client knew at the time.

Example in practice

A pre-existing warrant explains why your client fled when police arrived. Use its date to test the prosecution's inference.

Protecting another person

A client may hide information to protect someone else. That can make an account incomplete without proving the charged offense.

How to use itAsk privately for the missing facts and verify them independently. Avoid building an alibi on an untested story.

Example in practice

Your client concealed a friend's presence. Camera footage confirms the friend was there; revise the timeline around verified facts.

A different uncharged act

A client may have done something unlawful without committing the particular crime on trial. Different acts need different proof.

How to use itSeparate evidence of the charged elements from unrelated misconduct and challenge improper character use.

Example in practice

Your client admits trespassing, but that does not by itself establish who stole the equipment.

Evidence destroyed for another reason

Deleting or discarding material may suggest concealment, but its meaning depends on what was removed, when, and why.

How to use itSeek backups and establish the timing. Do not assume the client's explanation is true without checking.

Example in practice

Messages were deleted before the charged event. Use backup timestamps to test whether deletion could have hidden that event.

Prior felony history & fear of authorities

A prior conviction or fear of police can affect behavior without proving a new offense. Prior convictions may have limited permitted uses under the applicable rules.

How to use itExplain relevant behavior with evidence and challenge attempts to turn history into a general guilt inference.

Example in practice

Your client avoided police after a prior conviction. Distinguish that fear from evidence tying them to the present offense.

Prosecution tactics to recognize

All five courtroom traps in the case data, with a clear response.

FRE 404(b) / Impermissible Character & Prior Bad Acts

Past-bad-act ambush

The prosecutor introduces unrelated misconduct to encourage the jury to infer guilt from character. A proper non-character use needs a separate analysis.

How to use itObject to the improper purpose under Rule 404(b), address Rule 403 if applicable, and request a ruling before more detail reaches the jury.

Example in practice

The prosecutor mentions an old unrelated arrest to portray your client as dishonest. Ask what permitted issue it proves.

FRE 611(a) / Argumentative & Assuming Facts Not in Evidence

Loaded, compound questions

Rapid questions can bundle disputed assumptions with several requested answers.

How to use itObject under the judge's control of questioning and request one clear question without an unproved premise.

Example in practice

“When you hid the weapon and wiped it clean, who helped?” assumes multiple disputed events. Ask the lawyer to establish those facts separately.

FRE 701 & FRE 702 / Speculative Lay Opinion on Guilt/State of Mind

Ordinary witness giving technical conclusions

A non-expert is pushed to supply scientific findings or an unsupported conclusion about intent.

How to use itObject to speculation or improper lay opinion. Ask what was personally observed and what requires qualified expert analysis.

Example in practice

A patrol officer calls a wound pattern proof of premeditation without medical expertise. Separate the observation from the conclusion.

Due Process / Giglio v. United States / Concealed Witness Inducement

Concealed benefits for a witness

A witness is presented as disinterested while promised money, immunity, or a reduced sentence is hidden.

How to use itSeek disclosure of the promises and use them to test credibility. Explain any resulting preparation problem to the judge.

Example in practice

The informant says they helped out of civic duty, but payment records show a reward. Ask about the payment and its conditions.

FRE 802 / Crawford v. Washington / Sixth Amendment Confrontation

Hearsay disguised as investigation background

A detective repeats an absent person's accusation and calls it an explanation of the investigation. Some genuine background uses are permissible, but the label does not authorize proving guilt through an absent accuser.

How to use itAsk why the statement is needed, object to hearsay when offered for its truth, and raise confrontation when testimonial accusations are involved.

Example in practice

“We arrested Alex because an informant said Alex planned the robbery.” Challenge the accusation instead of accepting the “background” label.

Investigation & action points

All eight investigation types. The AP figures come from the RUC data; findings still need context and a proper foundation.

RUC investigation · 1 AP

Phone location & timing records

Cell connections and device clocks can help test a timeline, but location estimates and timestamps have limits.

How to use itRequest carrier records, coverage information, and clock offsets; compare independent sources.

Example in practice

A tower record places the phone in a broad area, not at a particular door. Use the uncertainty when testing an asserted exact location.

RUC investigation · 1 AP

Dispatch logs & emergency audio

Emergency calls, computer-aided dispatch (CAD) logs, and radio traffic preserve what was reported and when officers acted.

How to use itCompare the original audio with later reports and check whether clocks align.

Example in practice

The report says police saw a weapon before stopping the car; dispatch audio suggests the description came afterward.

RUC investigation · 1 AP

Travel & access records

Transit taps, toll readers, and badge logs can support or contradict travel claims. They identify a card or vehicle more directly than its user.

How to use itVerify time settings and who could use the card or vehicle. Cross-check with cameras.

Example in practice

A badge logged entry at 9:10, but another person had borrowed it. Request video before assuming who entered.

RUC investigation · 1 AP

Medical, intake & shift records

Ambulance observations, hospital arrival notes, and sign-in records can provide independent anchors for injury and movement timelines.

How to use itCheck authorship, time settings, identity, and whether the entry reflects an observation or later recollection.

Example in practice

A hospital intake record places your client away from the scene. Confirm the actual arrival time and identity recorded.

RUC investigation · 2 AP

Find route & shift witnesses

Canvassing means contacting people who may have directly observed a useful part of the event.

How to use itAsk open questions, preserve their original words, and verify what they could see or hear.

Example in practice

A night clerk remembers the delivery van leaving before the alleged crime. Compare that account with a dated receipt.

RUC investigation · 2 AP

Locate independent video

Doorbells, shop cameras, vehicle cameras, and network video recorders (NVRs) may capture missing parts of the sequence.

How to use itPreserve original files, check clock accuracy, and record where the footage came from.

Example in practice

A nearby shop camera shows the first aggressor. Obtain the original recording and identify its owner for authentication.

RUC investigation · 2 AP

Biological & ballistics review

Review of DNA mixtures, injury patterns, and firearm marks can test a claimed forensic link. A match claim needs explanation of limits and possible error.

How to use itAsk for raw records, controls, custody history, and qualified review. DNA presence alone does not establish when or how it arrived.

Example in practice

A mixed DNA sample includes several people. Ask whether the result truly singles out your client and how strong the association is.

RUC investigation · 2 AP

Digital & instrument review

File hashes, access records, and instrument calibration help test data integrity and measurements. A hash is a file fingerprint; a matching hash does not prove its author or truth.

How to use itCompare original and analyzed files, account access, time settings, and machine calibration. GC-MS is a laboratory method for separating and identifying chemicals.

Example in practice

The analyzed drive image has a different hash from the collected image. Investigate the change before relying on extracted messages.

Reading exhibits critically

Every evidence anchor in the case data: what it may help prove and what to check.

Physical evidence

Discarded Heavy Work Glove

A glove may connect a person to an item or location, but ownership, transfer, and timing remain separate questions.

How to use itCheck recovery location, custody, DNA interpretation, and innocent handling.

Example in practice

Your client once used the shared glove. Ask what ties its presence to the crime rather than earlier work.

Documentary evidence

Point-of-Sale Register Receipt

A register receipt may help establish a transaction or timeline, rather than reliably identify everyone present.

How to use itVerify the transaction, register clock, payment method, and who obtained it.

Example in practice

A 9:15 receipt supports a purchase time only after the register clock is checked.

Digital evidence

Low-Resolution Forecourt CCTV Snippet

A low-resolution camera clip may show movement while leaving identity uncertain.

How to use itObtain the full original recording, check timestamps, and avoid conclusions beyond the image detail.

Example in practice

The clip shows a dark coat but no face. Explain why many people fit that description.

Physical evidence

Serrated Utility Box Cutter

A cutting tool may be capable of causing an injury without being the tool actually used.

How to use itCheck injury compatibility, traces, handling, and the recovery context.

Example in practice

A box cutter was common workplace equipment. Ask what specifically connects this one to the wound.

Documentary evidence

Handwritten Shift Manifest & Notes

Shift notes may establish duties or observations, but handwritten information needs an author and context.

How to use itIdentify the writer, when notes were made, and whether they record firsthand facts.

Example in practice

A note was added after the incident. Ask whether it reflects a memory rather than a contemporaneous entry.

Documentary evidence

Equipment Maintenance Sign-Off Card

A maintenance card records inspections or repairs, but a signature is not conclusive proof every check occurred.

How to use itVerify the signer, procedure, underlying work, and equipment identity.

Example in practice

A card lists a successful check, while repair logs document a recurring fault. Compare both records.

Digital evidence

Transit Badge Keycard Scan Log

A keycard scan logs use of a credential, not necessarily the identity of its holder.

How to use itCheck sharing, duplicate cards, clock accuracy, and matching video.

Example in practice

Another worker borrowed the badge. Ask for an independent identification of the entrant.

Digital evidence

Patrol Dashcam Pre-Event Buffer Clip

A dashcam pre-event buffer may preserve events before manual recording starts, sometimes with limited audio.

How to use itInspect the original clip, buffer coverage, missing audio, and device timing.

Example in practice

The officer describes a spoken threat, but the buffer has no audio. Do not treat silence in that clip as proof no words were spoken.

Forensic evidence

Field Presumptive Chemical Test Vial

A field chemical test is a preliminary screen that may have false positives.

How to use itRequest confirmatory testing and inspect storage, contamination, and test instructions.

Example in practice

A field kit reacts positively but the lab identifies a lawful material. Use the confirmatory result to challenge the initial inference.

Documentary evidence

Intermodal Bolt Seal Manifest

A container seal manifest tracks seals, cargo, and handling rather than proving a particular person's knowledge.

How to use itCompare seal numbers, transfer signatures, opening records, and cargo access.

Example in practice

A seal number changes in transit. Ask who opened or resealed the container.

Forensic evidence

Hospital Emergency Toxicology Panel

A toxicology panel can identify tested substances, but timing, concentration, and clinical context affect interpretation.

How to use itCheck sample time, test scope, medication history, and expert limits.

Example in practice

Medication given in the emergency department may explain a result. Compare administration and collection times.

Forensic evidence

Autopsy Anatomical Wound Schematic

An autopsy diagram records injury locations; interpretation of mechanism, sequence, and intent requires an adequate expert basis.

How to use itCompare the diagram with the report, photographs, and the examiner's limits.

Example in practice

The diagram shows one entry wound but does not establish who fired. Separate medical findings from identity.

Digital evidence

Extracted Encrypted Messaging Log

A messaging extraction may show communications, but account identity, missing context, and file integrity matter.

How to use itVerify the original source, participants, full thread, timestamps, and extraction method.

Example in practice

A threatening line is followed by a clear discussion of a video game. Read the complete thread before inferring a real threat.

Digital evidence

Remote Server Bastion Auth Log

A bastion server access log records connections through a controlled gateway. Credentials and network addresses are clues to attribution.

How to use itCheck shared accounts, compromised access, time settings, and independent device evidence.

Example in practice

A login used your client's account from an unfamiliar device. Investigate compromise before assigning authorship.

Forensic evidence

Laboratory Gas Chromatograph Calibration Readout

A gas chromatograph calibration record helps show whether an instrument measured reliably at the relevant time.

How to use itCompare calibration dates, standards, drift, maintenance, and the specific sample run.

Example in practice

The instrument drifted outside its accepted range before testing. Ask whether that affected the reported result.

Documentary evidence

Automated Clearinghouse Wire Trace

An automated clearinghouse (ACH) or wire trace shows a payment path, but not automatically who authorized it or why.

How to use itTrace origin, approval, beneficiary ownership, access, and any reversal.

Example in practice

Money reached a supplier account. Check the contract and approval chain before describing it as a personal payoff.

Building a closing argument

All five verdict guides. A weakness may defeat a greater charge while leaving a lesser offense for consideration.

Violent crimes: identity & the first aggressor

Focus on whether the accused was reliably identified and whether the force was unlawful rather than justified.

How to use itConnect visibility, timing, physical presence, and the threat sequence to the charged elements.

Example in practice

Combine poor identification conditions with video showing the other person's initial attack. Explain how each affects a required finding.

Financial cases: dishonest intent

A financial loss alone does not prove deliberate fraud. Good faith and genuinely innocent mistakes can undermine intent; following a boss's orders is not a blanket defense.

How to use itUse contemporaneous instructions, approvals, and disclosures to test the claimed intent.

Example in practice

An email openly flags a bookkeeping mistake before money changes hands. Explain why that weakens an inference of deliberate deception.

Cyber cases: attribution & data integrity

A technical trace must be linked to the defendant and interpreted reliably. A shared network or account can create uncertainty, but does not automatically defeat attribution.

How to use itBring together account access, device evidence, timing, integrity checks, and alternative access supported by facts.

Example in practice

The login came through a shared account, and device evidence points elsewhere. Explain the gap between account activity and personal responsibility.

Drug cases: knowledge, control & distribution purpose

Shared premises can weaken an inference of knowing control. Personal-use evidence may challenge distribution intent while leaving possession at issue.

How to use itAddress possession and distribution separately. Joint possession is possible, so shared access is a fact to evaluate rather than an automatic defense.

Example in practice

Evidence suggests personal use but not sales. Argue the distribution element separately from whether possession was proved.

Overcharging & manslaughter: the proper mental state

Lack of planning or legally recognized mitigation may defeat a greater homicide charge without proving innocence of all homicide offenses. Felony-murder theories require separate analysis.

How to use itMatch your argument to the actual charge and any lesser-offense instructions. Identify exactly what is missing or mitigated.

Example in practice

Evidence supports a sudden confrontation but no planning. Explain why that matters for first-degree murder and assess any lesser charge independently.
